Refrigeration System Performance Analysis

Measuring the performance of a vapour-compression refrigeration unit two ways — and checking they agree

The challenge

How efficient is this refrigeration unit, really?

A refrigeration unit moves heat from a cold space to a warmer one using four components: compressor, condenser, expansion valve and evaporator. Five lab trials recorded pressures, temperatures, flow rates and the electrical energy used.

The aim was to turn those readings into the unit's real performance — heat removed, heat rejected, work input and coefficient of performance (COP) — and to confirm the result with an independent graphical method.

My role

What I did

  • Analysed the vapour-compression cycle and the role of each of its four stages.
  • Calculated heat rejected (QH), heat absorbed (QL), work input and COP for all five trials from the measured data.
  • Plotted each trial on the R-12 pressure–enthalpy (P-h) chart to find enthalpies and recompute the same values graphically.
  • Compared both methods trial by trial and calculated the error percentage for QH, QL and COP.
  • Recommended design changes to raise the cycle's COP.
Method

Two methods, one answer

  1. Understand the cycle

    Low-pressure vapour is compressed, condensed to a high-pressure liquid, expanded through a valve, and evaporated again while absorbing heat from the cooled space.

  2. Experimental method

    From the condenser water flow and temperature rise, the energy meter timing and the refrigerant data, I calculated QH, QL, work input and COP for each trial. Experimental COP ranged from 3.55 to 4.46.

  3. Graphical method and comparison

    Each trial was plotted on the R-12 P-h chart to read enthalpies. The two methods agreed within an average of 5.24%, with differences explained by heat leakage and reading accuracy during the experiment.

Results

  • Average COP of about 3.97 across both methods — an accepted value for a refrigeration cycle of this type.
  • Experimental and graphical results agreed within 5.24% on average.
  • Proposed improvements: add a flash chamber to separate liquid from vapour, and a heat exchanger between the condenser and expansion valve.

Lessons learned

  • Checking a result with a second, independent method builds real confidence in it.
  • Small deviations come from the real world — heat leakage and instrument readings — not the theory.
  • COP is a direct way to judge, and improve, the energy efficiency of a system.
